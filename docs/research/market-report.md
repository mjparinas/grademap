# Sell BC parents clarity, not more drills

The opening for a parent-facing, curriculum-aligned K-7 app in Canada is real but narrow, and price is not the way in. **BC has about 401,000 K-7 students in roughly 275,000 families**, with flat-to-declining enrolment. English Canada outside Quebec adds about **1.75 million families with a child aged 6-14**. Reaching 1% of BC families at the recommended price would bring in about **C$330K a year**, so BC can prove the model but cannot carry the business. The well-known competitors each leave a different gap. IXL is the only large paid product that claims BC alignment, but parents widely describe its scoring as punitive. Khan Academy is free but follows US course order. Prodigy aligns its Canadian content to Ontario only and sells memberships by marketing them to children. StudyPug is BC-aligned and cheap, but small. The largest unmet need is turning BC's 2023 proficiency-scale report cards into actions a parent can take: in a Leger poll, **64% of BC parents could not identify what "Emerging" means**, and Grade 7 numeracy results have slipped. The founder is right to drop ads, and the reasons go beyond parent dislike: platform rules, Canadian and Quebec law, and simple arithmetic all rule them out. The best scheme is a **parent-gated annual family subscription at C$119.99/yr (C$14.99/mo) for up to four children**, with a 30-day trial, a genuinely useful free report-card decoder, a free classroom version for teachers, and later add-ons (a French Immersion tier at C$149.99/yr, a summer math pass, tutor referrals, school licences). One assumption needs revisiting early. Canada is about **65% iOS**, so an Android-first launch reaches only about a third of parents unless it comes with a web parent portal and a quick follow-on iOS release.

## Roughly 275,000 BC families form a beachhead, not a business

BC's Ministry headcount shows **401,320 K-7 students in 2025/26**: 341,012 in public schools and 60,308 (15%) in independent schools. Enrolment peaked the year before at 405,421 and is now edging down, because this year's kindergarten class (43,709) is about 18% smaller than Grade 7 (53,030) ([BC Data Catalogue](https://catalogue.data.gov.bc.ca/dataset/7114187e-305c-4a79-acbb-3294ba0f53c8)). StatCan's tax-filer family file counts at least **280,560 BC families with a child aged 6-14** (2023), including 146,630 in the Vancouver CMA alone ([StatCan 39-10-0041-01](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/t1/tbl1/en/tv.action?pid=3910004101)). At roughly 1.45 children per family, that works out to about 275,000 BC families with an elementary-age child. That figure is a derived estimate, not a published count. The concentration in Metro Vancouver means a launch can lean on dense, local channels: schools, PACs and parent communities.

Several BC sub-segments look like higher-intent buyers. Independent schools hold 15% of K-7 students, and their share keeps growing while public kindergarten intake shrinks. BC households also spend more on education than the national average: **C$2,130 vs C$1,658 per household in 2023**, and C$92 vs C$67 on "other educational services" ([StatCan 11-10-0222-01](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/t1/tbl1/en/tv.action?pid=1110022201)). French Immersion enrols **34,300 BC K-7 students (8.5% of elementary)**. Districts from Sooke to Chilliwack still run kindergarten lotteries because teacher supply, not demand, is the constraint ([BC Data Catalogue](https://catalogue.data.gov.bc.ca/dataset/7114187e-305c-4a79-acbb-3294ba0f53c8); [SD62](https://sd62.bc.ca/node/68)). English Language Learners are **18.5% of BC elementary students** (74,237), which points to a large newcomer-parent audience that may need the curriculum explained in plain language. Home-based learners form a smaller, structural segment. About **11,750 K-7 students attend online-learning schools** as their primary school, two-thirds of them independent, and **4,477 children are registered homeschoolers**, about 1.8 times the pre-pandemic level ([BC Data Catalogue: homeschool](https://catalogue.data.gov.bc.ca/dataset/d378b0ba-6fa2-4f7f-a08c-97c697979ec3)). Online-learning schools must follow the BC curriculum. Their per-student resource allowances could make them a reimbursable channel, but the allowance amounts and eligibility rules were not verified.

Nationally, Canada had **3.30 million K-7 students and 3.72 million K-8 students in 2023/24**. Ontario has 1.23 million K-7 students, Quebec 0.75 million, Alberta 0.46 million and BC 0.40 million ([StatCan 37-10-0007-01](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/t1/tbl1/en/tv.action?pid=3710000701); [StatCan 37-10-0173-01](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/t1/tbl1/en/tv.action?pid=3710017301)). Families with a child aged 6-14 number at least 2.25 million: 845,340 in Ontario, 310,230 in Alberta and 500,770 in Quebec ([StatCan 39-10-0041-01](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/t1/tbl1/en/tv.action?pid=3910004101)). Alberta is the logical second province because it is the only large market whose 5-12 population is still growing, from 484,793 in 2024 to 491,573 in 2026 ([StatCan 17-10-0005-01](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/t1/tbl1/en/tv.action?pid=1710000501)). Quebec should come last, for reasons covered below.

Top-down data adds little. Statista's 2022 model put Canada's entire Education app category at **€144M**, made up of €77.5M in-app purchases, €64.2M advertising and €2.3M paid apps. That covers all ages and is dominated by language and adult learning ([Statista](https://www.statista.com/outlook/amo/app/education/canada/)). Tutoring-market estimates conflict wildly across vendors. The most-cited usage figure, that about a third of Canadian parents have paid for tutoring, dates from 2007-2015 surveys ([Convivium](https://www.convivium.ca/articles/here-comes-the-24-7-classroom/)). Bottom-up arithmetic is more honest. The table below applies the recommended C$119.99 family price to paid penetration scenarios. It is my calculation, not a forecast.

| Market (families) | 0.5% paid | 1% paid | 3% paid |
|---|---|---|---|
| BC (~275,000) | 1,375 families, C$165K gross | 2,750, **C$330K** | 8,250, C$990K |
| English Canada excl. Quebec (~1,750,000) | 8,750, C$1.05M | 17,500, **C$2.1M** | 52,500, C$6.3M |

Net revenue after the 15% store fee is about 85% of gross; in BC at 1%, that is about C$280K. The lesson is that BC validates willingness to pay and conversion mechanics, but a team-sustaining business needs English Canada. That makes the cost of localizing to each new province a core strategic variable.

### Android-first reaches only about a third of Canadian devices

The founder's Android-first plan runs against the installed base. StatCounter shows **iOS at 64.87% of Canadian smartphone traffic and 64.63% of tablet traffic** in September 2026, with Android at about 35% ([StatCounter mobile](https://gs.statcounter.com/os-market-share/mobile/canada); [StatCounter tablet](https://gs.statcounter.com/os-market-share/tablet/canada)). Tablets are the most-used device for Canadian children aged 2-6 (62%) ([MTM Jr. 2025](https://mtm-otm.ca/_files/NewsAlert/Exemple%20MIC%2021-08-2025%20(1).pdf)), and older MTM data shows Apple leading tablet ownership in families with young children ([Media in Canada](https://mediaincanada.com/?p=82758)). StatCounter measures page views, not devices, so it probably overstates iOS somewhat, but the direction is clear. Android-first is a reasonable engineering choice only if three things go with it: a cross-platform codebase, a **web parent portal at launch** (which also enables cheaper web billing, see below), and an iOS release within the first school year. Otherwise most BC parents who hear about the app at a PAC meeting will be unable to install it.

## Incumbents split into punitive, free-but-foreign and kid-monetized

The Canadian parent sees a price ladder that runs from free (Khan, Zearn, Ontario's TVO services, Quebec's Alloprof), through C$7-15/month apps, to C$20-30/month for IXL's top tier, and then jumps to C$150-260 per subject per month at Kumon. The table summarises the main players at verified CAD prices where available.

| **IXL** (US) | C$12.95/mo one subject, C$19.95 Math+ELA, C$24.95 All Access on web ([IXL CA](https://ca.ixl.com/membership/family/pricing)); App Store IAPs C$27.95-29.95 | Claims BC Learning Standards K-12 ([IXL BC](https://ca.ixl.com/standards/british-columbia/math)) | Punitive SmartScore, child distress, per-subject/per-child pricing |
| **Khan Academy / KA Kids** (US nonprofit) | Free ([KA](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/202260114-Does-it-cost-money-to-use-Khan-Academy)) | 2015 BC/ON mapping for Gr 4-6 only; US course order | Self-directed, confusing for younger kids, little parent guidance |
| **Prodigy** (Toronto) | App Store CA C$74.99 / C$114.99 / C$154.99 per year by tier ([App Store CA](https://apps.apple.com/ca/app/id950795722)); web billed in USD | Ontario only, Gr 1-8 ([Prodigy](https://www.prodigygame.com/main-en/math-curriculum-standards/)) | Pay-to-win, upsell aimed at kids, 2021 FTC complaint |

**IXL** is the incumbent to beat, and its weakness is how it feels to use, not what it covers. Its Canadian site lists C$12.95-24.95 per month in CAD and claims alignment to BC Learning Standards from K to Grade 12. All Access works out to about C$300 a year if paid monthly, and Apple's in-app prices are higher still ([IXL CA pricing](https://ca.ixl.com/membership/family/pricing); [App Store CA](https://apps.apple.com/ca/app/ixl/id693689912)). Common Sense Media aggregates **1,650 parent reviews** describing a "frustrating scoring system" that "demotivates students" and causes "emotional distress" ([CSM](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/website-reviews/ixl/user-reviews/adult)). On Trustpilot IXL scores **1.1/5 from 788 reviews**, with complaints that one wrong answer erases 15 points after slow gains of 2-3 ([Trustpilot CA](https://ca.trustpilot.com/review/ixl.com)). Sentiment depends on where you look: the iOS app holds 4.33 stars from 19,380 Canadian ratings, which suggests many families tolerate it. IXL argues SmartScore "is not a percentage" and measures consistency ([IXL blog](https://blog.ixl.com/?p=10804)). But the volume and emotional intensity of the complaints make this the most exploitable weakness in the market.

**Khan Academy** sets the price expectation at zero. It is a nonprofit, with about US$90M of contributions in its latest reported year ([Impala](https://impala.digital/public/profiles/26-1544963/financials/income-statement)). It has little that is Canadian. A 2015 partnership mapped only Ontario and BC Grades 4-6 math ([KA blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/the-learning-partnership-helps-bring-khan-academy/)). A Vancouver School Board page notes that Khan "generally follows the outline of the United States' education system" ([VSB](https://www.vsb.bc.ca/templeton/resources-junior-math)), and Khan Academy Kids aligns to Head Start and Common Core and tops out around Grade 2 ([HomeschoolFox](https://homeschoolfox.com/curriculum/khan-academy-kids)). Watch one signal: **Khanmigo Parent, at US$4/month, is US-only for now** ([KA support](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/tr/articles/25921448458893)). Khan is moving toward the parent layer, and a Canadian rollout would compress the space for a paid parent product.

**Prodigy**, the best-funded Canadian player (C$159M Series B, [BetaKit](https://betakit.com/edtech-startup-prodigy-education-raises-159-million-cad-series-b-round/)), shows both what works and what to avoid. Free classroom use drives parent memberships, and Prodigy runs no third-party ads. But Canadian content is mapped to Ontario only, web customers are billed in USD, and in 2021 a 22-group coalition asked the FTC to investigate it. They alleged kids saw "up to four times more advertisements than math exercises" for its own memberships, and that paid perks carried into classroom play ([Fairplay](https://fairplayforkids.org/feb-19-2021-advocates-to-ftc-prodigy-math-game-preys-on-kids-and-families/)). Prodigy itself said **more than 95% of registered users had not paid** ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/ncna1258294)), a useful sense of how few free users a freemium funnel converts.

**StudyPug** is the closest direct competitor, and it shows that "BC-aligned" alone is not a moat. It is BC-based, claims K-7 alignment down to BC content-elaboration codes, and charges from C$7.50/month for up to five children ([StudyPug](https://www.studypug.com/try-free/curriculum/ca/bc/math/grade1)). Its traction is modest and the product is video-led rather than parent-guidance-led. The public sector also stands out. Ontario funds free TVO Mathify tutoring ([TVO](https://tvo.me/?p=5083)). Quebec funds Alloprof up to **C$21M over 2022-25**, and Alloprof has a parents' section that already explains report cards ([Quebec Gazette](https://www.publicationsduquebec.gouv.qc.ca/fileadmin/gazette/pdf_encrypte/lois_reglements/2024F/82811.pdf); [Alloprof](https://www.alloprof.qc.ca/en/parents/articles/report-cards-and-school-system/understanding-your-childs-report-card-k1472)). **BC has no visible K-7 equivalent.** That gap is an opportunity and a risk at once. It leaves room for a BC product and creates a possible partner or customer in government, but a provincially funded free service would undercut a paid one overnight.

## Most BC parents cannot decode their child's report card

In July 2023 BC replaced K-9 letter grades with a four-level Provincial Proficiency Scale: Emerging, Developing, Proficient and Extending. Districts state that letter grades "cannot be requested or used" for K-9 ([gov.bc.ca](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/education-training/k-12/administration/legislation-policy/public-schools/student-reporting); [SD23](https://www.sd23.bc.ca/student-reporting)). The Ministry's parent one-pager mostly reassures. It says "Emerging doesn't mean your child is unsuccessful" and "Proficient is the goal", but it never tells a parent which skill is missing or what to practise ([BC Ministry](https://curriculum.gov.bc.ca/sites/curriculum.gov.bc.ca/files/pdf/reporting/K-12-student-reporting-one-pager-for-parents-and-caregivers.pdf)). A 2024 Leger poll for the Fraser Institute found that 57% of Canadian parents considered "Emerging" unclear and only 28% could identify its meaning, while **98% said clear assessment matters** ([Global News](https://globalnews.ca/news/10828759/bc-parents-letter-grades-descriptive-grading-schools/)). Among BC parents, who have lived with the scale, **64% could not correctly identify "Emerging"** ([Fraser Institute](https://www.fraserinstitute.org/commentary/bc-parents-confused-by-governments-new-descriptive-grading)). The Fraser Institute opposed the reform and commissioned the poll, and some educators argue the scale is no vaguer than letter grades ([The Tyee](https://thetyee.ca/News/2023/09/01/BC-End-Letter-Grades-Report-Cards/)). Still, the need shows up in the market. Districts invent driving analogies to explain the scale ([SD60](https://www.prn.bc.ca/report-card-renovations-proficiency-scale-a-driving-comparison/)), and tutoring firms publish "report card decoded" posts to win BC parents ([Tutriva](https://tutriva.com/blog/bc-proficiency-scale-report-card-decoded/)). There is a further complication: the Grade 4 and 7 FSA reports on yet another scale (Emerging, On Track, Extending) ([SD83](https://www.sd83.bc.ca/fsa-information)). A product can stay neutral in the letter-grade debate and still serve both camps, because the gap is in understanding, not in wanting feedback.

The worry behind the confusion is real. Since 2021-22, **roughly 40% of BC Grade 7 students have not met FSA numeracy expectations**, compared with just under half before the pandemic ([The Tyee, 2025](https://thetyee.ca/News/2025/10/07/Math-Reading-Scores-Declining-BC/)). Richmond's district report says the provincial Grade 4 literacy average "dropped considerably" in 2024-25 ([SD38](https://sd38.bc.ca/app/uploads/2026/02/SD38-ESLR-Report-2025.pdf)). Canada's PISA math score fell 35 points between 2003 and 2022 ([Globe and Mail](https://arc-dev.theglobeandmail.com/canada/article-math-scores-of-canadian-students-are-declining-raising-concern-about)). The experts The Tyee quotes keep naming the same remedies: more practice and math-fact automaticity, which BC's curriculum does not require by Grade 5. BC began mandatory kindergarten reading screening in 2025-26 ([BC Gov News](https://news.gov.bc.ca/releases/2025ECC0025-000851)), so parents will soon receive more data about their child that they need help interpreting.

Research also says what parents should do with that information, and it is not to sit down and teach. A meta-analysis of 50 studies found academic socialization (talking about expectations and goals) had the strongest link to achievement, while direct homework help was inconsistent and sometimes negative ([Harvard GSE](https://www.gse.harvard.edu/ideas/usable-knowledge/09/10/parental-involvement-puzzle)). Children of math-anxious parents learned less when those parents helped often ([Maloney et al.](https://sites.temple.edu/cognitionlearning/files/2015/09/Maloney-et-al-2015.pdf)). Light-touch coaching works better: the READY4K trial sent parents three short texts a week and raised early literacy by about 0.11 SD ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20659.pdf)). Screen time is the boundary. In an Angus Reid survey, 89% of parents saw devices as a useful teaching aid, but 46% worried about how much time kids spend on them ([Angus Reid](https://angusreid.org/screen-time-kids/)), so a short, capped daily session sells better than an endless one. French Immersion parents face a sharper version of the same problem. Schools tell them they "will not need to speak French" ([Winnipeg SD](https://www.winnipegsd.ca/victoria-albert/french-immersion)), yet they cannot see whether their child is keeping up, and Richmond FI parents told an SFU researcher they felt "unprepared and uncertain" ([SFU thesis](https://summit.sfu.ca/_flysystem/fedora/2023-09/etd22578.pdf)). The biggest evidence gap is that **no Canadian survey measures what parents will pay** for any of this, so the price points below are informed hypotheses to test, not established demand.

## Compete on clarity and kindness, and let price follow

The positioning that holds up against all of this evidence is **"the parent's companion to the BC curriculum."** That means four things. It turns this term's report card into named skills. It shows what is being taught this term in plain English (and French). It runs about ten minutes a day of kind, foundational practice in math facts and phonics. And it gives the parent one coaching script a week. That puts it in a different category from both incumbents, which is what lets it avoid competing on price.

Against **IXL**, the mistake would be to position as a cheaper IXL. Price stays in the background; the wedge is emotional safety and usability, because the most frequent and most emotional complaints are about scoring and layout, not about content coverage. The concrete commitments are these: progress never drops because of a single error, typos and misclicks are forgiven, every miss comes with an explanation, cancellation takes one tap, and the honest CAD family price covers every sibling. IXL's per-subject, per-child structure (plus about US$40/yr per additional child in the US, [Time4Learning](https://time4learning.com/vs/ixl/cost.html)) then works as a contrast the parent notices without being told. The app should also report in the school's own vocabulary (Emerging, Developing, Proficient, Extending in BC) and produce a shareable "evidence of learning" snapshot for parent-teacher conferences, rather than an IXL-style score that competes with the teacher. The BCTF already objects that outside assessments add little to teachers' judgement ([The Tyee](https://thetyee.ca/News/2025/10/07/Math-Reading-Scores-Declining-BC/)), and a product teachers resent loses the classroom channel.

Against **Khan**, competing on content volume or price is pointless, because Khan wins both. Khan's own reviewers name what it lacks: parents describe a "confusing layout, lack of adequate explanations" and frequent need for adult guidance ([CSM](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/website-reviews/khan-academy/user-reviews/adult)), and the course order is American. The answer is to sell answers instead of a library. The free tier must deliver something Khan does not, namely the BC report-card decoder plus a placement diagnostic, so the comparison a parent makes is "free US practice" against "a plan for my child's BC report card." The paid tier adds the weekly plan, coaching scripts modelled on READY4K-style nudges, progress reports in report-card language, and multi-child management. Against **Prodigy**, the differentiator is trust: no ads, no prices or locked rewards shown to children, no pay-to-win, and no in-class advantages for paying families. Against **StudyPug**, it is guidance and emotional design rather than videos. Two further trust signals cost little and matter in BC: Canadian data hosting and a "no data selling" pledge. A credible approach to Indigenous perspectives also matters, built with partners such as FNESC rather than as a badge ([FNESC](https://www.fnesc.ca/first-peoples-principles-of-learning/)).

This positioning also keeps expansion cheap. Math localizes at low-to-moderate cost, because Manitoba, Nova Scotia and PEI built their curricula on the shared WNCP framework ([Manitoba](https://www.edu.gov.mb.ca/k12/cur/math/framework_k-8/background.pdf); [Nova Scotia](https://curriculum.novascotia.ca/sites/default/files/documents/curriculum-files/Mathematics%201%20Guide%20%282019%29.pdf)). One skill graph mapped to about four outcome sets (BC, Ontario 2020, Alberta 2022 and WNCP-derived) covers most of English Canada. The report-card decoder is the distinctive layer, and it is mostly copy and mapping work: Ontario uses Levels 1-4 plus EQAO ([EQAO](https://eqao.com/infographic-provincial-standard)), and Quebec uses percentages weighted 20/20/60 by term ([Quebec.ca](https://quebec.ca/en/education/preschool-elementary-and-secondary-schools/ministerial-examinations-evaluation-learning/report-card-weightings-wording)). That suggests an expansion order of Alberta (growing, with a new K-6 curriculum that leaves parents disoriented), then the WNCP provinces, then Ontario (largest, but with free TVO tools). **Quebec should come last.** There, Alloprof already offers free, French-first parent explainers, and Quebec bans commercial advertising aimed at children under 13. French belongs in the product as a bridge for Immersion families in BC and Ontario (an English parent view of French practice), not as a reason to enter Quebec.

## Monetize parents directly: ads fail on law, platforms and arithmetic

### The founder is right about ads, for more reasons than parent dislike

The founder's instinct holds, but the stronger arguments are structural. On the platform side, Apple's Kids Category guidelines say apps for kids "should not include third-party analytics or third-party advertising" and allow contextual ads only with human-reviewed creatives ([Apple guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Google Play requires child-directed apps to use only self-certified Families ad SDKs with G-rated, child-directed treatment ([Google Play](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9900633)), and its Teacher Approved reviewers explicitly score the "appropriateness of ads" ([Play Console](https://play.google.com/console/about/programs/teacherapproved)). On the legal side, the federal privacy commissioner's position is that children under 13 "cannot meaningfully consent" ([OPC](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/about-the-opc/what-we-do/consultations/completed-consultations/consultation-children-code/report_children-code_2026/)). Quebec's Law 25 requires parental consent to collect data on children under 14, and its Consumer Protection Act ss. 248-249 **ban commercial advertising directed at children under 13** outright ([Gowling WLG](https://gowlingwlg.com/insights-resources/articles/2026/rules-for-marketers-and-advertisers-when-advertising-to-children-in-canada)). That rules out a national ad tier unless it is geo-fenced. US users would bring the amended COPPA rule, compliance date April 22, 2026, which requires separate parental consent for targeted ads ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule)).

The arithmetic is just as decisive. Even at the general-audience banner rate of about US$1.22-1.30 eCPM ([Playio](https://blog.playio.co/mobile-game-ecpm-benchmarks-2026)), and kid-safe contextual inventory pays less than that, 10,000 daily users seeing four banners a day earn about **US$14,600 (roughly C$20K) a year**. If just 5% of those families paid C$119.99, they would bring in about **C$60K** (my calculations, at an assumed 1.37 CAD/USD). At 140 million monthly users, Duolingo still earns only **about 7% of revenue from ads** ([Duolingo Q2 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001562088/000162828026053299/q2fy26duolingo6-30x26share.htm)). Evidence on what parents think is thinner than the founder might assume. Researchers found ads in 95% of apps used by young children ([Michigan Medicine](https://michiganmedicine.org/health-lab/advertising-kids-apps-more-prevalent-parents-may-realize)) and manipulative design in about 80% ([Radesky et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9206186/)). Yet a dated 2019 game-industry survey found half of parents preferred in-game ads to paying ([Interpret](https://interpret.la/?p=9758)), and no current Canadian survey exists. The fairest reading is that some parents tolerate ads in games. For a premium curriculum product, though, ads destroy the trust promise that justifies the price. The only acceptable ad-like uses are cross-promotion of the founder's own products and parent-facing marketing behind a parental gate.

### Benchmarks favour a trial-gated annual family plan

Education is the most premium-tolerant app category, and it converts slowly. RevenueCat's 2026 data shows a **median annual price of US$44.99, the highest of any category**, and annual plans make up 59-66% of education subscriptions. Only 28.5% of paid conversions happen on the day of install, against 50.6% across all apps ([RevenueCat Education](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps-2026-education)). Hard paywalls convert downloads to paid at a median **10.7% versus 2.1% for freemium** ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/hard-paywall-vs-freemium)), with about the same one-year retention. Trials of 17-32 days convert best, and annual plans convert 86% better with 30-day trials, while monthly plans gain little beyond about two weeks ([SaaStr](https://saastr.com/what-17000-subscription-apps-tell-us-about-free-trial-length-annual-plans-convert-86-better-with-30-day-trials-monthly-tops-out-at-two-weeks-and-ai-apps-hit-a-wall-at-16-days)). Adapty adds two warnings. In education, **23.5% of trials start more than 31 days after install**, so a free layer that keeps parents around until a report card arrives pays off. And education apps price Canada at **0.7 times the US**, which is the race to the bottom this product must avoid ([Adapty](https://adapty.io/blog/markdown/education-app-subscription-benchmarks.md)). Family plans are standard: Epic covers four children ([HomeschoolFox](https://homeschoolfox.com/curriculum/epic)), and Duolingo's family plan reached about 20% of its subscribers ([MarketBeat](https://www.marketbeat.com/earnings/reports/2024-8-7-duolingo-inc-stock)).

Store economics are simple. Apple's Small Business Program and Google Play subscriptions both take **15%**. Canada has no alternative-billing or link-out right on either store ([Apple Small Business Program](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/small-business-program/); [Google Play](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/13821247?hl=en)). The founder may still sell directly on the web to parents who arrive through schools, PACs, email and search, and may email existing users about web purchase. At C$119.99 that nets about **C$116 through Stripe versus about C$102 in-store**. Selling direct makes the founder the merchant of record, so BC's Bill 4 (in force August 1, 2026) applies directly. For terms over 60 days it requires cancellation at any time, refunds for renewals cancelled within 15 days, and renewal notice 30-60 days ahead. Quebec's Bill 10 adds trial-end notices and an easy cancellation mechanism ([Torys](https://www.torys.com/en/our-latest-thinking/publications/2026/07/provincial-governments-continue-to-tighten-consumer-protection-laws)). Building those courtesies in from day one turns compliance into a selling point against IXL's cancellation complaints.

Several rules make the scheme work. Offer a **30-day trial on annual and 14 days on monthly**. Show all commerce in a parent area behind a parental gate, and never show children prices, locked rewards or "ask your parents" prompts. Do not discount. Acquisition should come from the free decoder and the classroom version, and the price-sensitive Canadian education market should be met with visible value, not cuts. Before committing to C$119.99, run a paywall test with arms at **C$99.99, C$119.99 and C$149.99**. Time the main annual-plan push for mid-August to September, when ClassDojo historically earns its best month ([Appfigures](https://appfigures.com/resources/insights/20230908?f=5)), and trigger lifecycle emails around each of BC's five yearly reporting moments. Treat PACs as a distribution channel, not as customers. BC gaming grants of C$20 per student explicitly cannot fund curriculum materials ([BC Gaming Grants guide](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/sports-recreation-arts-and-culture/gambling/grants/archived-2023-guide-pac.pdf)), but parent nights and newsletters reach exactly the right buyers. Hold a one-time "grade pack" in reserve for subscription-averse parents rather than launching with it, because ongoing reporting and sibling coverage fit recurring revenue better. Finally, BC's Interactive Digital Media Tax Credit refunds **25% of eligible BC labour** ([Gov. of BC](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/taxes/income-taxes/corporate/credits/interactive-digital-media)). It lowers build cost, but it is not revenue.

## Conclusion

The defensible asset here is not a question bank, which IXL, Khan and StudyPug already have, but a **translation layer**: report-card language mapped to skills, and skills mapped to one parent action this week. That layer is cheap to localize province by province, it fits the research on how parents actually help, and it is the one thing the free incumbent and the paid incumbent both lack. It also changes the pricing question. A product that answers "what does Developing in Numeracy mean, and what do we do tonight?" is bought on a different basis from "more practice", which is why C$119.99 for the whole family can hold against a free Khan without discounting.

The near-term risks are about sequencing more than demand. BC enrolment is shrinking and an iOS-majority audience will not reach an Android-only app, so the first year should combine a cross-platform build, a web parent portal and a paywall price test, with Alberta prepared as the second province. The two developments most likely to reshape the opportunity are Khan bringing its parent product to Canada, or BC funding an Alloprof-style public service. The first argues for building parent trust and BC credibility quickly. The second is equally a reason to have a FIPPA-ready, Canadian-hosted product that a ministry could buy rather than replace.
